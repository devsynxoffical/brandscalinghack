This MER calculator gives you the one efficiency number that platform dashboards cannot inflate. Enter total revenue from all channels and total marketing spend from all channels, and it returns your marketing efficiency ratio. Add new-customer revenue and it also returns new-customer MER, often called aMER, which shows how efficiently you are buying growth rather than reselling to existing buyers.

Meta, TikTok and Google each claim credit for overlapping sales, so adding up their reported ROAS usually overstates results. MER sidesteps the argument by comparing what came into the bank with what went out on marketing.

What is MER in ecommerce?

MER, or marketing efficiency ratio, is total store revenue divided by total marketing spend across every channel. It ignores platform attribution and shows how hard your whole budget is working. A store with $250,000 in revenue and $50,000 in marketing spend has an MER of 5.0, meaning each marketing dollar returned $5.

How to use the MER calculator

Pick one period, usually a week or a month, and pull every figure for that same period. Mixing a calendar month of revenue with a 30-day rolling window of spend will distort the result.

  • Total revenue (all channels): net sales from Shopify or your store platform for the period, including organic, email, direct and paid traffic. Use net of refunds if you can.
  • Total marketing spend (all channels): Meta, TikTok, Google, influencer fees, affiliate commissions and any other paid acquisition spend for the same period.
  • New-customer revenue (optional): revenue from first-time customers only. Shopify reports first-time versus returning customer sales, or you can export it from your analytics tool.

The MER formula with a worked example

MER = total revenue / total marketing spend

New-customer MER (aMER) = new-customer revenue / total marketing spend

Example: in October a supplement brand did $250,000 in total revenue and spent $50,000 across Meta, TikTok and Google. MER is $250,000 / $50,000 = 5.0. Of that revenue, $150,000 came from first-time customers, so new-customer MER is $150,000 / $50,000 = 3.0.

The gap between the two tells a story. Returning customers produced $100,000 that the marketing budget did not have to buy again. If MER looks healthy but aMER is falling month after month, the store is leaning on its existing list and acquisition is getting harder.

How to read the result

There is no universal good MER. The right number depends on your gross margin, how much of revenue is repeat business and whether you are in a growth or profit phase. A useful way to set your own target is to work backwards from contribution margin: if your break-even ROAS is 2.0x, an MER well above 2.0 means marketing is covering itself with room to spare.

Watch the trend more than the single reading. If you raise spend by 25% and MER drops only slightly, the extra budget is finding real buyers. If MER falls sharply, the new spend is mostly buying sales you would have made anyway. Our glossary entry on MER at /glossary/mer explains how it relates to blended ROAS.

MER and the advertising-to-sales ratio

The advertising-to-sales ratio is marketing spend divided by revenue, written as a percentage, which makes it the inverse of MER. A store with $250,000 of revenue and $50,000 of marketing spend has a 5x MER and a 20% advertising-to-sales ratio (50,000 / 250,000). The calculator above shows both, so you can use whichever figure your finance team or investors report.

The two answer the same question from opposite sides: MER says how much revenue each marketing dollar brings in, the ratio says how much of each sales dollar goes back into marketing. To compare it with your margins, the ratio has to stay below your contribution margin before marketing, or every sale loses money once ads are paid.

Common mistakes

MER is simple to calculate, which makes it easy to calculate inconsistently. Most problems come from mismatched dates or a definition of marketing spend that changes from month to month. Write your definition down once, share it with anyone who reports on the business, and keep using it. The mistakes below are the ones that most often make MER look better or worse than the business really is, and each one is easy to avoid once you know to look for it.

  • Leaving influencer, affiliate or agency creative fees out of marketing spend, which flatters MER.
  • Comparing a sale week with a normal week without noting the discount. Promotions raise revenue and can hide a weakening aMER.
  • Treating MER as a channel metric. It measures the whole budget, so use platform data to decide where to move money within it.
  • Using gross sales before refunds, which overstates revenue in categories with high return rates.

Frequently asked questions

What is the advertising to sales ratio?

The advertising to sales ratio is total marketing spend divided by total revenue, shown as a percentage. It is the inverse of MER: a 5x MER equals a 20% advertising to sales ratio. Keep it below your contribution margin before marketing, otherwise each sale loses money after ad spend.

How do you calculate MER?

Divide total revenue from all sales channels by total marketing spend from all channels over the same period. If your store made $250,000 and you spent $50,000 on Meta, TikTok, Google and other paid marketing, your MER is 5.0. Keep the dates identical for both figures so the ratio is accurate.

What is the difference between MER and ROAS?

ROAS usually refers to revenue a single ad platform attributes to its own campaigns, divided by spend on that platform. MER uses all store revenue and all marketing spend, so it does not depend on attribution. ROAS helps you optimise inside a channel, while MER tells you whether the overall budget is paying off.

What is aMER or new-customer MER?

aMER divides revenue from first-time customers by total marketing spend. It removes repeat purchases, which marketing often did not need to buy, so it shows how efficiently you are acquiring new buyers. A store with $150,000 of new-customer revenue on $50,000 of spend has an aMER of 3.0.

Should email and SMS costs count as marketing spend in MER?

It depends on what you want to measure. Many brands count only paid acquisition spend, because email and SMS mostly drive repeat orders. Others include Klaviyo and agency costs for a fully loaded view. Pick one definition, write it down and use it every month so trends stay comparable.

How often should I check MER?

Weekly is a good rhythm for most DTC brands spending meaningful budgets, with a monthly review to set targets. Daily MER swings too much because of order timing and payday cycles. Look at a rolling seven-day figure when you change budgets, and compare it with the same period before the change.
