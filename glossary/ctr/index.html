What Is CTR? Click-Through Rate in Paid Ads

Home / Glossary / CTR

By Gaurav Kapoor, Founder, Brand Scaling · Updated 2026-11-05

What is CTR? CTR stands for click-through rate: the share of ad impressions that lead to a click. If 1,000 people see your ad and 15 click it, your CTR is 1.5%. It is the clearest early signal of whether your creative is doing its job.

Like CPC, Meta reports CTR (all) and CTR (link click-through rate). CTR (all) includes every interaction, such as likes and profile taps. Link CTR counts only clicks to your site. For an ecommerce brand, link CTR tells you how many people were interested enough to visit the product page.

What is CTR in advertising?

CTR, or click-through rate, is the percentage of people who click your ad after seeing it. It equals clicks divided by impressions, multiplied by 100. CTR shows how well your creative and offer grab attention. A higher link CTR lowers cost per click and usually signals that the ad message resonates with the audience.

CTR (Click-Through Rate)
CTR is the percentage of ad impressions that result in a click, calculated as clicks divided by impressions and multiplied by 100.

Formula: CTR = (clicks / impressions) x 100

CTR formula and how to calculate it

The formula is CTR equals clicks divided by impressions, multiplied by 100 to give a percentage.

Worked example (illustrative numbers): an ad gets 125,000 impressions and 1,875 link clicks. CTR is 1,875 divided by 125,000, multiplied by 100, which equals 1.5%.

CTR has a direct effect on cost. With $2,500 spent, CPC is about $1.33. If a new hook lifts CTR to 2.0% on the same impressions, clicks rise to 2,500 and CPC falls to exactly $1.00, a drop of roughly 25% without any change to budget or targeting. That is why creative testing focuses so heavily on the opening seconds of each ad.

Also watch thumbstop rate on video: 3-second video views divided by impressions. It shows whether the hook works before anyone decides to click.

What a healthy CTR depends on

CTR varies a lot by format, placement and audience, so the most useful comparison is against your own account. Track the CTR of your current best ads and judge new creative against that baseline rather than a generic industry figure. A new ad that beats your control on CTR and holds conversion rate is a candidate to scale. The main factors that move CTR are:

  • Hook strength: the first frame or headline decides whether people stop scrolling.
  • Audience temperature: retargeting warm visitors usually produces a higher CTR than cold prospecting.
  • Placement: feeds, stories, reels and the audience network behave differently.
  • Offer and call to action: a clear reason to click now lifts CTR.
  • Frequency: as the same people see an ad repeatedly, CTR tends to decline.

How to improve CTR

Start with hooks. Test several openings for each winning angle, such as naming the problem, showing the result, a surprising demonstration or a real customer reaction. Keep everything else the same so you know which change caused the lift.

Make the product and benefit obvious within the first seconds. Use native-looking formats, including UGC, because polished ads that look like ads are easier to scroll past. Add on-screen text for people watching without sound. Refresh creative on a schedule rather than waiting for performance to collapse, since CTR is often the first metric to show fatigue. And match the ad to the landing page, so the people who click find what they expected and actually buy.

Common CTR mistakes

  • Reporting CTR (all) as if it were link CTR, which overstates real interest.
  • Chasing CTR with misleading hooks that attract clicks but not purchases.
  • Comparing cold and retargeting CTR as if they should be equal.
  • Killing ads on CTR alone before checking cost per purchase.
  • Testing too many variables at once, so a CTR change cannot be explained.

Related metrics

CTR links CPM and CPC: CPC equals CPM divided by clicks per thousand impressions. Falling CTR alongside rising frequency is the classic sign of creative fatigue. After the click, conversion rate takes over and decides whether that traffic becomes revenue. Our Meta ads agency page explains how we use these signals to decide which ads to scale.

A simple weekly routine ties these together. Sort active ads by spend, then note link CTR, frequency and cost per purchase for each. Ads with high spend, falling CTR and rising frequency are due for a refresh. Ads with strong CTR but weak cost per purchase point to a landing page or offer problem rather than a creative one. That split tells you where to spend the next week of effort.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between CTR and link CTR?

CTR (all) counts every click on the ad, including likes, comments, shares, profile taps and expanding the caption. Link CTR counts only clicks that send people to your website. For ecommerce, link CTR is the version that matters because it measures real store visits. CTR (all) can look healthy even when very few people reach your product page.

Does a high CTR mean my ad is working?

Not always. A high CTR shows the ad grabs attention, but the real test is whether those clicks become purchases at an acceptable cost. Clickbait or curiosity hooks can lift CTR while conversion rate falls. Judge an ad on cost per purchase and ROAS, and use CTR as an early signal of creative strength.

How long should I wait before judging CTR on a new ad?

Give each ad enough impressions to produce a stable number, often a few thousand at minimum, and at least a day or two of delivery. Early CTR can swing a lot. Compare new ads against your best current ad over the same period, and look at cost per purchase before making scaling decisions.

Why is my CTR dropping over time?

The most common reason is creative fatigue: the same people have seen the ad several times and stopped reacting. Check frequency alongside CTR. If frequency is climbing and CTR falling, introduce new hooks or angles. Seasonal changes, audience saturation and competitor activity can also pull CTR down even when the ad itself has not changed.

Does CTR affect Meta ad costs?

Indirectly, yes. Meta favours ads that people engage with, and higher CTR means more clicks from the same impressions, so cost per click falls. That flows through to cost per purchase if conversion rate holds. This is why strong hooks and fresh creative are among the most effective ways to control Meta ad costs.
