Contribution Margin: The Profit Metric DTC Brands Scale On

Home / Glossary / Contribution margin

By Gaurav Kapoor, Founder, Brand Scaling · Updated 2026-11-05

Contribution margin is what remains from revenue after you pay every cost that rises and falls with sales. For an ecommerce brand, that usually means product cost, shipping, fulfilment, payment fees, discounts and returns. Many operators also calculate it after marketing spend, which shows the true profit from paid acquisition.

It matters more than gross margin for DTC brands because gross margin ignores costs like shipping and fees that can quietly eat a large share of each order. Contribution margin is the number that tells you whether scaling ads is building profit or just building revenue.

What is contribution margin in ecommerce?

Contribution margin is the money left from each sale after subtracting all variable costs, such as product cost, shipping, payment fees, packaging and often ad spend. It shows how much each order contributes toward fixed costs and profit. DTC brands use it to set break-even ROAS and decide how far paid ads can scale.

Contribution margin (CM)
Contribution margin is revenue minus all variable costs, such as product, shipping, fees and often marketing, showing what each sale contributes to fixed costs and profit.

Formula: Contribution margin = revenue - variable costs (as %: contribution margin / revenue)

Contribution margin formula and how to calculate it

The formula is contribution margin equals revenue minus variable costs. As a percentage, divide that result by revenue. Some teams split it into levels: contribution margin before marketing, and contribution margin after marketing.

Worked example (illustrative numbers): an order brings in $100 of revenue. Product cost is $30, shipping is $8, payment fees are $3 and pick and pack is $4, for $45 of variable costs. Contribution margin before marketing is $55, or 55%.

Now add ad spend. If the brand spends $35 in ads for each order, contribution margin after marketing is $20 per order, or 20%.

This number sets your break-even ROAS: 1 divided by 0.55 is about 1.82x. At a ROAS below 1.82x, each ad-driven order loses money before fixed costs. The break-even ROAS calculator does this for you.

What a healthy contribution margin depends on

There is no single target. A brand with high repeat purchase rates can accept a thin first-order contribution margin because later orders are far more profitable. A brand selling one-time purchases needs a healthy margin on the first order.

A useful exercise is to build a simple per-order profit table for your best-selling product, then repeat it for your most common bundle and a typical discounted order. Seeing the three side by side often shows that a promotion or bundle is far more or far less profitable than it appears. What matters most:

  • Product cost and pricing power: the gap between what you pay and what customers will pay.
  • Shipping and fulfilment: heavy or bulky products carry more cost per order.
  • Discounting: frequent promotions lower contribution margin on every discounted order.
  • Returns: high return rates turn apparently profitable orders into losses.
  • Repeat purchase rate: retention lets you invest more of first-order margin into acquisition.

How to improve contribution margin

Look at every variable cost line. Negotiate product costs as volume grows, review packaging, and compare fulfilment and shipping providers. Small savings per order add up quickly at scale.

Raise average order value with bundles and upsells, since fixed per-order costs such as pick and pack are spread across more revenue. Replace blanket discounts with targeted offers, such as free gifts or bundles, that protect price. Reduce returns with clearer product information and sizing. On the marketing side, better creative and conversion rate lower ad cost per order, which lifts contribution margin after marketing. One dropshipping store we scaled reached a 56% profit margin over 21 days by keeping these costs in check while growing revenue.

Common contribution margin mistakes

  • Using gross margin to set ROAS targets and forgetting shipping, fees and returns.
  • Ignoring discount costs when reporting promotional periods.
  • Treating fixed costs like salaries as variable, or variable costs as fixed.
  • Calculating contribution margin once and never updating it as costs change.
  • Scaling ad spend while contribution margin dollars stay flat or fall.

Related metrics

Contribution margin sets your break-even ROAS and the minimum blended ROAS or MER you need. CAC and LTV only make sense on a margin basis, so contribution margin is the link between them. AOV changes it directly, because larger orders spread fixed per-order costs.

A useful monthly habit is to report contribution margin in dollars, not just as a percentage. A falling percentage can be fine if total contribution dollars are rising fast, and a rising percentage can hide a business that is shrinking. Track both, and judge scaling decisions on the dollars.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between gross margin and contribution margin?

Gross margin subtracts only the cost of goods sold from revenue. Contribution margin also subtracts other variable costs such as shipping, fulfilment, payment fees, discounts and returns, and often marketing. For DTC brands, contribution margin is the more realistic measure because those extra costs can be large and they rise with every order.

Should contribution margin include ad spend?

Both versions are useful. Contribution margin before marketing tells you how much each order can afford to spend on acquisition and sets break-even ROAS. Contribution margin after marketing shows how much profit paid growth is actually producing. Many brands track both, sometimes labelled CM2 and CM3, and use the after-marketing figure to judge scaling.

How do I use contribution margin to find break-even ROAS?

Divide 1 by your contribution margin before marketing, expressed as a decimal. If 55% of revenue remains after variable costs, break-even ROAS is 1 divided by 0.55, about 1.82x. Below that, each ad-driven order loses money. Your target ROAS should sit above break-even to cover fixed costs and leave profit.

What lowers contribution margin the most for DTC brands?

The usual culprits are heavy discounting, rising shipping costs, high return rates and paying too much to acquire each order. Payment fees and packaging matter less individually but add up. Reviewing each cost line per order, rather than looking at totals, usually reveals where the biggest leak is and which fix will help most.

Can a brand grow with a low contribution margin?

It can if repeat purchases are strong, because later orders cost little to acquire and lift the overall margin per customer. Without good retention, a low first-order contribution margin limits how much you can spend on ads and how fast you can scale. Improving pricing, AOV or costs usually comes before pushing more budget.
