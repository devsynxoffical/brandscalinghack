What Is MER? The Marketing Efficiency Ratio Explained

Home / Glossary / MER

By Gaurav Kapoor, Founder, Brand Scaling · Updated 2026-11-05

What is MER? MER stands for marketing efficiency ratio, and it measures how much total revenue your store generates for every dollar you spend on marketing across all channels. It is sometimes called blended ROAS, although some teams keep the two separate by counting only ad spend in blended ROAS and all marketing costs in MER.

MER matters because ad platforms each claim credit for the same sales. Meta, Google and TikTok all report their own ROAS, and the totals rarely match what landed in Shopify. MER cuts through that by using the one number nobody can inflate: the revenue in your bank account.

What is MER in ecommerce?

MER, or marketing efficiency ratio, is total store revenue divided by total marketing spend over the same period. It ignores platform attribution and asks one question: for every dollar spent on marketing, how many dollars of revenue came in? DTC brands use it as the top-level health check for paid growth.

MER (Marketing Efficiency Ratio)
MER is total store revenue divided by total marketing spend for the same period, showing revenue earned per marketing dollar regardless of platform attribution.

Formula: MER = total revenue / total marketing spend

MER formula and how to calculate it

The formula is simple: MER equals total revenue divided by total marketing spend for the same date range. Use net revenue from your store (after refunds) and include every paid channel. Many brands also include agency fees, creator payments and software tied directly to acquisition, so decide your definition once and keep it consistent.

Worked example (illustrative numbers): a brand spends $50,000 on Meta, $15,000 on Google and $10,000 on TikTok in a month, for $75,000 of total marketing spend. Shopify shows $300,000 in net revenue for the same month. MER is $300,000 divided by $75,000, which equals 4.0. Every marketing dollar returned four dollars of revenue.

A useful variant is new customer MER, sometimes written as aMER. If $180,000 of that revenue came from first-time buyers, new customer MER is $180,000 divided by $75,000, or 2.4. This shows how efficiently spend is buying growth rather than harvesting repeat customers who would have bought anyway.

What a healthy MER depends on

There is no universal good MER. The number you need comes from your own unit economics, not from an industry average. A brand with 75% gross margin can grow profitably at a far lower MER than a brand with 40% margin.

The practical way to set a target is to work backwards. Calculate contribution margin before marketing as a percentage of revenue, then divide 1 by it to find break-even MER. A brand keeping 50% of revenue after product, shipping and fees breaks even at a MER of 2.0. Your growth target then sits above that floor, with room for fixed costs and profit.

  • Contribution margin: the more you keep after product, shipping and fees, the lower the MER you can afford.
  • Repeat purchase rate: strong retention lets you accept a lower first-order MER because returning revenue lifts it later.
  • Growth stage: a brand pushing into new markets often runs a lower MER on purpose while it buys customers.
  • Revenue mix: a large email or wholesale base inflates MER without any change in ad efficiency.
  • Seasonality: Q4 and launches shift both revenue and costs, so compare like periods.

How to improve MER

MER improves when revenue rises faster than spend. In practice that means working on both sides of the ratio rather than just cutting budget, which often lowers revenue at the same rate.

Raise conversion rate and average order value first, because they lift revenue from traffic you already pay for. Bundles, post-purchase upsells and clearer product pages all help. Then improve the cost side by refreshing creative before it fatigues, removing campaigns that only retarget existing buyers, and making sure your Conversions API setup gives Meta clean purchase data to optimise on. Finally, build retention flows in email and SMS so repeat revenue grows without new ad spend.

Common MER mistakes

  • Changing the definition month to month, for example adding agency fees in one report and not the next.
  • Comparing MER across periods with very different promotions, such as BFCM against a quiet February.
  • Reading MER daily. It is noisy at that level; weekly and monthly views are more reliable.
  • Treating a high MER as automatically good. A very high MER can mean you are underspending and leaving growth on the table.
  • Ignoring new customer MER, which hides whether paid media is still buying new buyers.

Related metrics

MER sits at the top of a stack of numbers. Blended ROAS is its closest relative and often used interchangeably. Customer acquisition cost tells you what each new buyer costs, and contribution margin tells you what MER you can afford before you lose money. Frequently asked questions

Is MER the same as ROAS?

Not quite. ROAS is usually reported by one ad platform and only counts sales that platform attributes to its ads. MER divides all store revenue by all marketing spend, so it does not depend on attribution at all. Blended ROAS is close to MER, and many brands use the terms interchangeably as long as the definition stays consistent.

Should MER include agency fees and tools?

It can, and many brands prefer it that way because those costs exist only to acquire customers. The important thing is consistency. If you include agency fees, creator payments or attribution software in one month, include them every month. Otherwise changes in MER reflect a change in accounting rather than a change in marketing performance.

How often should I check MER?

Weekly and monthly are the most useful views. Daily MER swings with order timing, payday effects and promotions, so it can trigger bad decisions like cutting a campaign that is working. Review weekly trends for direction and use the monthly number for planning budgets and comparing against your break-even target from contribution margin.

What is a good MER for a DTC brand?

A good MER is one that keeps you above break-even after product costs, shipping, fees and marketing. That number is different for every brand because margins and repeat rates differ. Work out your contribution margin first, then calculate the minimum MER you need, and set a growth target above it rather than borrowing an industry figure.

Why does my MER drop when I scale ad spend?

Each extra dollar usually reaches people who are a little less likely to buy, so efficiency tends to fall as spend rises. That is normal. The question is whether total profit still grows. If revenue and contribution margin dollars keep rising while MER drifts down gently, scaling is working. A sharp drop usually points to creative fatigue or audience saturation.
